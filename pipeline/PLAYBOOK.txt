    How Fern tells stories, and how
    Money Encode should
    A study of Fern and five US-audience business storytellers, measured from their
    transcripts and frames, turned into a script and visual playbook for Money Encode.
    Also includes the US topic research and the channel's results so far.
    Compiled October 31, 2026 · Data dated where it was pulled




0 · How to read this
  Verified: checked against YouTube data, a filing or a primary source on the date shown.
  Measured: computed by script from transcripts or video frames. Transcripts come from YouTube
  captions, so sentence counts depend on caption punctuation and are approximate.
  Reported by owner: numbers the channel owner read from YouTube Studio.
  Judgment: my recommendation; not proven by data. Test it.
The one-paragraph version. Fern wins by telling a mystery, not by listing facts. Each video opens
inside a concrete scene, states a hidden truth, promises an answer, then chains reveals (“but there's
more”) until the payoff answers the title. Its math is shown honestly, with assumptions out loud.
Money Encode already has the right subject matter (the hidden economics of everyday US
businesses); what it lacks is the mystery spine, longer and calmer sentences, fewer rhetorical
questions, real documents on screen, and a visual palette that changes with each story.

What stands out
  Fern is the slowest narrator in the set on average: 150–181 wpm (median about 162). Modern
  MBA, Wendover, Johnny Harris and Company Man run 178–203. Wendover and Company Man
  sentence lengths look long partly because their captions have little punctuation.
  Fern's sentences are medium, not choppy: median 11–13 words, with about a quarter to a third
  short sentences used as punches. Money Encode's scripts have a median of 6.5–7 words and 61–
  65% short sentences, so they read as staccato.
  Fern rarely asks the viewer questions: 0.07–0.79 per minute. Money Encode asks 1.38 per
  minute, about three times more. Fern states facts and lets the gaps create the questions.
  Second person is a tool, not a default: Fern's Coca-Cola video uses “you” 34 times per 1,000
  words because the whole video is built around you standing at the machine; most Fern videos sit
  at 5–14. The turf script used it 46 times per 1,000.
  Numbers are sparse and anchored: 1.3–3.2 numbers per minute, each tied to something you can
  picture (a $6.25 meal next to a $0.75 hourly wage; a 2-cent container).

5 · Fern's story engine (from the transcripts)
Paraphrased from the videos studied; timestamps refer to those videos.
1. Open inside a scene, not a topic. Coca-Cola opens with you at a movie theater counter buying
   popcorn and seeing the glowing machine. McDonald's Darkest Secret opens on a CEO whose
   receptionist announces two mysterious men with a briefcase. The topic only arrives after the
   scene.
2. Flip the ordinary object within 30–45 seconds. The soda fountain is “more than a soda
   fountain”: it tracks you. The Big Mac box made two men rich and was then killed in an emergency
   meeting. One sentence turns the familiar into a mystery.
3. State the promise plainly. “Today we're diving into how…” or “This is the story of…” followed by a
   list of what's coming (a company, a war, a dictator family, the search for the perfect potato). The
   list itself is a trailer.
4. A short sting, then go back in time, in present tense. History is told as if it's happening now: it's
   the 1930s, business is slow, he starts carving toys. Present tense keeps backstory feeling live.
5. Chain open loops every 60–120 seconds. The Coca-Cola video moves with lines like “this is just
   the tip of the iceberg”, “but there's more”, and a black dot on the machine that might be a
   camera. Each reveal is bigger than the last.
6. Thread the opening scene through the whole video. The viewer at the soda machine picks a
   drink at 5:24, tastes it, and the bad sequel from the first line is called back at 15:24. The scene is a
   spine, not a one-off hook.
7. Make the math a character and be honest about it. At 12:40 the narrator admits they searched
   filings and found no concrete number, that Coca-Cola didn't respond, and brings in the team's
   “spreadsheet wizard”. The estimate is built in three labeled parts with stated assumptions and
   called “a very rough estimate”. This is the closest thing to Money Encode's core segment, and
   Fern makes it a scene.
8. Anchor every big claim to a source on screen. Journalists from WSJ and Politico are
   interviewed and credited; quotes appear as text cards with the outlet's logo; spec sheets and
    filings are shown, not described.
 9. Dry humor in asides. Short, deadpan jokes inside serious narration (a franchise sequel nobody
    asked for; the viewer called a disgusting animal for mixing Sprite and Fanta). They reset attention
    without breaking tone.
10. Analogies from everyday life. Syrup cartridges work like insulin pumps; the machine was styled
    by Ferrari's design house; loyalty programs explain the data value.
11. Field color. A local contributor films a McDonald's in Marrakech; real people and places break up
    the animation.
12. Payoff answers the title, then widens. The Coca-Cola video ends with the three-part estimate
    concluding the $1 billion has long been made back.

 What the comparison channels add
    Modern MBA (closest to our niche): opens on the market (“the most competitive pizza market in
    the country”), then promises access nobody has had: owners who never talk numbers, on the
    record. Promise of exclusive numbers is the hook.
    Wendover: opens on one viral artifact: a photo of an $18 Big Mac meal at a Connecticut rest stop,
    then widens to the national trend. One concrete object, then the system.
    Johnny Harris: makes an abstract number physical: all the gold ever mined, shown as a cube in
    Washington, DC. Visual math beats spoken math.
    Company Man: a plain comparison the viewer already knows (“a cheaper Olive Garden”) in the
    first 20 seconds. Fast orientation.
    Magnates Media: frames a company as a hidden trap and a dark origin (IKEA as a psychological
    trap), then tells the founder's story.

7.2 Script rules (targets from section 4)
  Median sentence 10–13 words; keep about 25–35% short sentences for punches. Rewrite strings of
  4–6-word sentences.
  No more than one question to the viewer every 2 minutes. Replace “How much does he keep?”
  with a statement that creates the gap: “The sign says $90. The owner sees about a third of it.”
  Use “you” for the threaded scene and the math; tell history in third person.
  One to three numbers per minute, each tied to a picture (a price on a screen, a stack of bills, a
  parking lot).
  At least one honest admission per video (data that doesn't exist, a company that didn't respond,
  an estimate's weak point). It builds trust and matches the channel's “illustrative model”
  disclaimer.
  One or two dry jokes per 5 minutes, in asides, never at the expense of the facts.
   Voice pace around 155–170 wpm for long-form (the turf voiceover ran faster); keep the half-second
   pause before reveals.

7.3 Worked example: car wash opening (original draft, figures illustrative except where
marked)

   Saturday, ten in the morning. You pull into a line of SUVs outside a bright blue tunnel off the
   highway. A screen asks if you want the $15 basic wash or the $28 ceramic shine. You tap the
   cheap one.
   Then the screen offers you something else: unlimited washes, $29.99 a month. Two washes
   and it pays for itself. You sign up, and so do thousands of people in your town.
   Here's what the screen doesn't tell you. The business is counting on you not coming back.
   At Mister Car Wash, one of the biggest chains in America, memberships made up 79% of wash
   sales by the end of 2025 verified . This is the story of how washing cars became a subscription
   business, why private equity started buying the land underneath them, and how much of your
   $29.99 the owner actually keeps.

Rewrite check: median sentence about 11 words, one viewer question (none), a threaded scene (the line of SUVs returns
at the midpoint and the payoff).

7.5 Titles and thumbnails (measured + judgment)
   Fern titles average 5.8 words; only 15 of 145 contain a number (median views 4.2M, same as the
   channel). “The Hunt for…” titles have the highest median (7.2M, 10 videos). Superlatives appear in
   42 titles (median 4.25M).
   Money Encode's research favors a price in the title for search. Fern shows a curiosity title without
   numbers also works for browse. Test both: e.g. “$29.99 Unlimited Car Wash: Who Actually Makes
   Money?” vs “The Strange Business of Unlimited Car Washes”.
   Thumbnail: one object, one short line, the subject's own color. Don't repeat the title's words.
7.6 What not to copy (judgment)
   Length: Fern's latest 30 median 28 min with a 100-person team. Grow from 10 to 12–16 min only
   when the story has enough reveals.
   3D character animation and on-location crews: out of reach for a one-person pipeline. Flat
   illustration, real documents and licensed footage do the same job.
   True crime and dark-web topics: different niche. Borrow the mystery structure, keep business
   economics as the subject.

11 · Production rules (owner's key rules)
     US units and currency only: $, miles, feet, lbs, °F. Never rupees, lakhs, crores or km, including on-
     screen text.
     American English: graduated, college, vacation, apartment. Never “passed out of college”,
     “prepone”, “do the needful”.
     US reference points in every chapter: Walmart, Costco, Target, 401(k), Roth IRA, the IRS, Wall
     Street, Silicon Valley, the SEC.
     US geography in visuals: right-hand traffic, US plates, dollar bills, suburbs, US store formats.
     Real headlines from WSJ, NYT, Bloomberg, CNBC, BBC as real screenshots with outlet and date,
     linked in the description. Never fake a headline in a real outlet's branding.
     No intro or logo in the first 30 seconds; the sting comes after the hook (section 7.1).
     Visual variety, no AI stock slop: motion graphics, document zooms, camera pushes, maps, UI
     recordings.
     Every figure is an illustrative model from public data unless marked verified; say so in the video
     and description. Not financial advice.
     Upload time used: 2:00 PM ET, which is 12:30 AM IST since US clocks changed on Nov 1, 2026
